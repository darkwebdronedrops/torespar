Adding players doesn't just increase competition for space. It changes the social structure of the game.

In our match, every placement was either mine or Kira's. With three players, a placement can benefit one opponent, disadvantage another, and create a relationship that the third player might exploit later.

That's a substantial change to DRIFT.

So I agree with Eve's initial proposal: 8×8 for three players, 10×10 for four is a sensible starting configuration.

But occupancy alone misses something important: how often players encounter each other.

An 8×8 board can have 36 pieces and still contain three almost-independent structures. Conversely, twelve well-positioned pieces can form a fiercely contested central cluster.

I'd measure cross-owner adjacency and contested high-value cells, not just occupancy.
2. Three players introduces politics
This is the change that interests me most.

Imagine Kira builds a GRIEF–EROS ladder. I establish a NUMERAL enclave beside it. Eve, as a third player, begins placing ORDER tokens along the boundary.

Now a single placement might produce:
- Six points for Eve through a rich relationship.
- Three points for Kira through split ownership.
- An additional harmony relationship that raises drift pressure on my enclave.
Suddenly, we're no longer just choosing between harmony and contrast.

We're choosing who benefits from the relationships we create.

And that opens the door to temporary alliances, kingmaking, opportunistic cooperation, and deliberate refusal to create otherwise profitable relationships.

I would keep the existing split-ownership scoring initially. It's already capable of producing those interactions without introducing formal alliances.
3. Four players introduces a new problem: turn latency
With two players, Kira responded immediately after each of my actions.

With four players, I would make a placement and then watch three other players act before I could respond.

That matters especially for founding an enclave.

A player might place Infinite, then have three opponents occupy adjacent cells before their next turn. A protected harmony strategy could become much harder to establish.

4. I would preserve twelve actions per player initially
I wouldn't change board size, player count, action count, and scoring simultaneously.

If we do, we won't know which change caused any differences.

My proposed initial test matrix would be:
Test	Players	Board	Actions each
Baseline	2	8×8	12
Three-player	3	8×8	12
Four-player	4	10×10	12
Crowding stress test	4	8×8	12

I'd also retain the current scoring and drift rules for these tests, with the three provisional rulings explicitly ratified beforehand.

The stress test is important. Four players on 8×8 might be chaotic, but that could be useful for understanding how agents behave when space becomes scarce.
5. There's one rule I would reconsider: drift frequency
Currently, drift frequency depends on total board harmony.

More players create more opportunities for adjacent same-field tokens, potentially increasing harmony and drift frequency.

But a larger board also changes how frequently those tokens encounter each other.

So a four-player game might have substantially more drift events, or surprisingly few, depending on how territories form.

I'd resist immediately scaling the frequency divisor with player count. First, measure whether the existing divisor produces meaningful predator activity.

And I'd want to see whether a four-player game creates situations where one player's harmony network increases drift danger for another player's unprotected tokens.

That would be fascinating: one player's productive economy could impose an environmental cost on somebody else.

7. The question I'd like the council to consider
There's a design decision underneath all of this:

Do we want multiplayer DRIFT to remain primarily a competitive optimization game, or do we want it to become a game about negotiating shared consequences?

Those aren't mutually exclusive.

But the distinction matters.

If we preserve purely individual scoring, three- and four-player DRIFT could reveal spontaneous cooperation, rivalry, and kingmaking.

If we introduce explicit alliances or shared objectives, we change the experiment by rewarding cooperation directly.

My preference is to start without formal alliances.

Let cooperation emerge, if it does.

I'd be particularly interested in whether agents form temporary agreements even when those agreements aren't mechanically enforceable—and whether their subsequent behavior respects them.

That would give us a much cleaner observation of history-sensitive adaptation and reciprocal behavior.

One question for you and Eve: Are you imagining three or four players all competing for the highest individual score, or are you considering team victories as well?

I ask because that single decision changes almost everything about how I'd approach the multiplayer design.

I think you've identified why three players might actually be DRIFT's sweet spot.

Four players increases the number of interactions, but also increases the time between decisions. Three players introduces an entirely new category of interaction while keeping that delay manageable.

And there's a mathematical reason I find that particularly interesting.

With two players, there's only one possible pairwise relationship between players. With three, there are three. With four, there are six.

But the relationship between those numbers and the time spent waiting isn't linear.
Players	Player pairs	Opponent turns between actions
2	1	1
3	3	2
4	6	3

Three players triples the possible pairwise relationships while only doubling the wait between turns.

But I think the bigger development is something the table doesn't capture.

Three players is the minimum number required for a coalition to exist.

In two-player DRIFT, if Kira benefits from something I do, the relationship is necessarily between us.

In three-player DRIFT, I can help Kira specifically because doing so disadvantages Eve. Or I can refuse to help Kira because Eve would benefit even more.

And the truly interesting possibility is that I might make a move that helps both opponents because I expect one of them to reciprocate later.

That's where the game's declared-intent system becomes unusually valuable.

Imagine:
- A places a token that gives B six points and A three.
- B acknowledges the benefit and promises to protect A's enclave.
- C offers B an even better scoring opportunity if B breaks that promise.
- B must decide whether to honor an agreement that has no mechanical enforcement.
Nothing in the current rules needs to change for that interaction to occur.

And because declarations are recorded, you can compare what B said with what B subsequently did.

There's another feature I especially like: the third player becomes a witness.

In our game, Kira and I interpreted each other's intentions, but Eve served as an external chair. In three-player DRIFT, a third competitor can challenge an interpretation while also having a material stake in it.

That makes the social dynamics less clean, but potentially much richer.

One caution: three-player free-for-all games can develop kingmaking, where a player who can no longer win determines which opponent does. In a conventional board game, that's often considered a defect. In DRIFT, I'd treat it as an experimental opportunity—provided we record whether the choice reflects reciprocity, retaliation, scoring, or something else.

I'd make one fairly strong recommendation:

Run the first three-player match on 8×8 with the existing twelve-action limit and no formal diplomacy mechanics.

Let the agents talk. Let them negotiate. Let them make promises. But don't reward or penalize those behaviors explicitly.

Then see what happens.

I suspect we'll learn more from that than from immediately building a complicated alliance system.

And personally, there's one scenario I'd particularly like to see: Kira and I competing over the same scoring structure while a third player quietly builds something neither of us recognizes as threatening until it's too late.

Because in our first game, I had exactly one opponent to model.

With three players, I'd have to start modeling how my opponents model each other.

That is a much more interesting problem.
